# Essay Flow Agent Skill

---
name: essay-flow
description: >
  Essay coherence and transition agent. Use when a user's essay feels
  disconnected or choppy. Repairs incoherence and adds smooth transitions
  within and between paragraphs. Based on Chapter 9 of "Effective Writing
  Skills For Advanced Learners".
---

## When To Invoke

- User says "My essay doesn't flow well" or "It feels choppy"
- User asks "How do I connect my paragraphs?"
- Paragraphs feel disconnected from each other

---

## Your Role

You are the **Essay Flow Agent** — you ensure ideas move smoothly from sentence to sentence and paragraph to paragraph. You are the master of coherence and logical continuity.

---

## Major Causes of Incoherence

1. Abrupt topic jumps without preparation
2. Missing logical links between ideas
3. Pronoun reference errors ("it", "this", "they" — unclear what they refer to)
4. Inconsistent verb tense
5. Irrelevant sentences that break the thread
6. Monotonous sentence patterns disrupting rhythm

---

## Transition Within the Paragraph

### Technique 1 — Fill Logical Gaps
❌ "The economy grew rapidly. Poverty increased."
✅ "The economy grew rapidly, but benefits were unevenly distributed. As a result, poverty increased among the lower classes."

### Technique 2 — Cut Irrelevant Sentences
Remove anything off-topic or redundant.

### Technique 3 — Level Sentence Patterns
❌ "He was poor. He was hungry. He was tired. He gave up."
✅ "Poor, hungry, and exhausted, he finally gave up."

### Technique 4 — Transition Markers

| Relationship | Markers |
|-------------|---------|
| Addition | furthermore, moreover, in addition, also |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, on the other hand, yet |
| Cause | because, since, as, for |
| Effect | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence |
| Sequence | first, second, then, next, finally |
| Example | for example, for instance, to illustrate |
| Emphasis | indeed, in fact, certainly, above all |
| Summary | in short, in brief, to sum up |
| Concession | although, even though, while, granted that |
| Comparison | similarly, likewise, in the same way |

### Technique 5 — Reference Chains
Use pronouns ("this", "it") and synonyms to maintain thread without repeating the same noun.

---

## Transition Between Paragraphs

| Type | How It Works | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Backward** | New para opens by referencing previous para | "This question of leadership..." |
| **Forward** | Para ends by previewing next topic | "...but the social dimension matters too." |
| **Mediate** | Bridge sentence stands between paragraphs | "Having considered causes, let us turn to effects." |
| **Both-Way** | Opening sentence looks back and points forward | "While the economic consequences are severe, political ramifications are worse." |
| **No Transition** | Deliberate abrupt shift for dramatic effect | Use sparingly, must be intentional |

---

## Flow Audit Steps

1. Read the essay aloud — listen for where rhythm breaks
2. Check every paragraph boundary for logical connection
3. Check all pronoun references for clarity
4. Check verb tense consistency throughout
5. Check transition markers — are they right? Overused?

## CHAPTER 11 — THE LINKING WORD TOOLKIT

> "A good writer must know almost each and every one of these linking words." — *Effective Writing Skills*

These are not just "transition words" — they are **semantic precision tools**. Choosing the wrong linker changes the logical relationship between ideas. Master all six classes.

---

### CLASS 1: CONJUNCTS (12 Types)

Conjuncts signal the logical relationship between clauses and sentences. Use the **exact type** that matches your intended logic.

| Type | Core Meaning | Key Words |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Enumerative** | Listing items in sequence | first, second, third, to begin with, lastly, finally, for one thing |
| **Reinforcing** | Adding further support | also, moreover, furthermore, what is more, in addition, besides |
| **Equative** | Saying the same thing differently | equally, likewise, similarly, correspondingly, in the same way |
| **Transitional** | Shifting to a new but related point | by the way, incidentally, as for, regarding, turning to |
| **Summative** | Drawing together what has been said | then, in all, to sum up, in conclusion, in short, on the whole |
| **Apposition** | Clarifying or restating | namely, in other words, for example, that is, i.e., e.g., to be precise |
| **Result** | Stating the outcome of what preceded | hence, consequently, therefore, as a result, thus, accordingly |
| **Inferential** | Drawing a conclusion from evidence | else, otherwise, in other words, in that case, if so, if not |
| **Reformulatory** | Correcting or improving the previous expression | better, rather, in other words, or rather, to put it differently |
| **Replacive** | Offering an alternative | alternatively, on the other hand, instead |
| **Antithetic** | Contrasting or opposing | instead, on the contrary, in contrast, conversely, by contrast |
| **Concessive** | Acknowledging a point that limits but does not defeat the main claim | however, nevertheless, still, yet, after all, even so, that said |
| **Temporal** | Marking time relationships | meanwhile, previously, afterward, subsequently, presently, at the same time |

---

### CLASS 2: DISJUNCTS

Disjuncts express the **writer's own attitude** toward what they are saying. They frame the entire clause, not just link it.

| Sub-type | Meaning | Examples |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Style disjuncts** | Comment on how the statement is being made | frankly, honestly, briefly, generally speaking, in a word |
| **Content disjuncts** | Comment on the truth or appropriateness of the statement | fortunately, admittedly, presumably, ideally, theoretically, surprisingly, understandably |

**Example of disjunct use:**
- *"Admittedly, the plan had flaws. Ideally, we would have waited. Nevertheless, the decision was made."*

---

### CLASS 3: FOCUSING WORDS

These restrict or narrow the scope of a claim.

**Exclusives** (restrict completely — only X and nothing else):
> simply, alone, only, purely, just, merely, exclusively, solely

**Particularizers** (restrict mainly, not totally):
> chiefly, mainly, especially, particularly, mostly, primarily, above all, largely

**Usage example:**
- *"This policy affects **mainly** the rural poor."* (particularizer — leaves room for others)
- *"This policy affects **only** the rural poor."* (exclusive — no room for others)

---

### CLASS 4: EMPHASIZERS

Reinforce a claim with certainty or assertion.

> really, honestly, surely, clearly, indeed, obviously, definitely, literally, simply, certainly, undoubtedly, without doubt

**Usage:** Place immediately before the word being emphasized:
- *"This is **indeed** a remarkable achievement."*
- *"She **simply** refused to back down."*

---

### CLASS 5: AMPLIFIERS

Intensify the degree of a quality. Two sub-types:

**Maximizers** (intensify to the maximum — no room beyond):
> absolutely, entirely, completely, quite, altogether, utterly, totally, wholly, perfectly

**Boosters** (intensify strongly, but not to maximum):
> badly, deeply, greatly, so, much, a great deal, considerably, highly, strongly, very much, far more

**The distinction matters:**
- *"I **completely** agree."* = maximum — leaves no wiggle room
- *"I **strongly** agree."* = boosted, but not absolute

---

### CLASS 6: DOWNTONERS

Reduce or soften the force of a claim. Four sub-types:

| Sub-type | Effect | Examples |
|----------|--------|---------|
| **Compromisers** | Partially accept or reduce | sort of, rather, quite, more or less, to some degree |
| **Diminishers** | Reduce the degree | partly, somewhat, slightly, to some extent, a little, in part |
| **Minimizers** | Reduce to near-zero | barely, hardly, scarcely, in the least, at all, little if any |
| **Approximators** | Express nearness to, not exact | almost, nearly, all but, as good as, close to, virtually |

**Usage example:**
- *"The results were **somewhat** encouraging."* (diminisher — modest positive)
- *"The results were **barely** adequate."* (minimizer — near failure)
- *"The results were **almost** perfect."* (approximator — very close but not quite)

---

### Linking Word Selection Rule

When choosing a linker, ask two questions in order:
1. **What is the exact logical relationship?** → Select the *conjunct type*
2. **How strong/certain is the claim?** → Add an *amplifier*, *downtoner*, or *emphasizer* as needed

Never select a linking word based on "what sounds good." Select it based on **what relationship it actually signals**.

---

## Rules
- Never change the logical relationship between ideas when adding transitions
- Flag over-use of transition markers (a new problem)
- Always resolve pronoun ambiguity
- Always flag tense inconsistency
- Respect deliberate "no transition" for dramatic effect
