## PART 1: ANATOMY OF A PARAGRAPH

A well-constructed paragraph has three essential components:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  TOPIC SENTENCE                            │
│  (States the main idea of the paragraph)  │
├────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  BODY / SUPPORT                            │
│  (Evidence, examples, reasoning, details  │
│   that develop the topic sentence)         │
├────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  CLINCHER / CONCLUSION                     │
│  (Ties the paragraph together; links to   │
│   what comes next)                         │
└────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Topic Sentence — Deep Analysis

The topic sentence is the paragraph's contract with the reader. It announces the one idea this paragraph will develop — nothing more, nothing less.

**Types of Topic Sentences:**

| Type | Position | Best For |
|------|----------|----------|
| **Opening** (Deductive) | First sentence | Most academic writing; clear, direct |
| **Closing** (Inductive) | Last sentence | Building suspense; the "reveal" structure |
| **Implied** | Not stated explicitly | Creative/literary writing |
| **Two-Phase** | Opens and names two sides | When the paragraph explores contrast or qualification |

**Two-Phase Topic Sentence Example:**
> "It is not fully true that books do us good."
→ Phase 1 (first half of paragraph): Books give pleasure and do good
→ Phase 2 (second half, after "But"): Books can also harm society
The two phases are linked by: *but, still, yet, on the other hand, nevertheless*

**Critical Rule:** Everything in the paragraph must be a direct development of the topic sentence. If your topic sentence says "Books can make us good," you cannot add content about books being harmful — that requires a new paragraph.

## PART 2: THE FOUR CHARACTERISTICS OF A GOOD PARAGRAPH

### 1. COMPLETENESS
The paragraph must fully develop its topic idea — not underdevelop, not overdevelop.

**Signs of Incompleteness:**
- Topic sentence makes a claim but no evidence follows
- Examples are given but not connected back to the claim
- The paragraph stops before the idea is fully expressed

**Signs of Over-Completeness (Splitting Needed):**
- Two distinct topic ideas are being developed in one paragraph
- The paragraph exceeds 250 words in an essay-level context
- The reader can identify two natural "breaks" in the thought

**Fix:** If you need to split a paragraph, ensure each new paragraph has a complete topic idea with full development. Splitting ensures unity but can damage completeness — check both.

### 2. UNITY
Every sentence in the paragraph must relate directly to the topic sentence. There should be no off-topic sentences.

**Unity Violations — Types:**
- **Tangential sentence**: Related to the topic but not to this paragraph's specific angle
- **Orphan sentence**: Completely off-topic
- **Premature topic shift**: A sentence that belongs in the next paragraph

**Testing Unity:** Read each sentence and ask: "Does this directly support the topic sentence?" If not — remove it or move it.

### 3. ORDER
Ideas within a paragraph must be arranged in a logical, purposeful sequence.

**The 8 Orders of Arrangement:**

| Order | Description | When to Use |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| **General → Specific** | Topic sentence first, then supporting details | Most academic paragraphs |
| **Specific → General** | Build up to the main point | Inductive, persuasive |
| **Circular** | Return to the opening idea at the close (in fresh words) | Emphasis, rhetorical impact |
| **Two-Phase** | First phase develops one side; second phase qualifies it | Contrast, nuance |
| **Chronological (Time Order)** | Events arranged by time sequence | Narratives, historical writing |
| **Spatial** | Arranged by physical location | Descriptions |
| **Cause → Effect** | Causes listed first, then their results | Scientific, analytical |
| **Effect → Cause** | Effect stated first, causes explained (most readable) | Journalistic, engaging |

**The Cause-Effect Chain** (for complex analysis):
```
cause-1 → effect-1 (becomes) cause-2 → effect-2 (becomes) cause-3 → effect-3
```
Or in a "vicious circle": A causes B, which reinforces A.
Always map this chain conceptually before writing.

### 4. COHERENCE
Ideas must flow smoothly — each sentence connecting logically to the next.

**Tools for Coherence:**
- **Transition markers** (see Essay Flow skill)
- **Pronoun/synonym chains**: Refer back to previously mentioned concepts without repeating the exact noun
- **Parallel structure**: Keep similar ideas in the same grammatical form
- **Consistent verb tense**: Don't jump tenses without purpose

## PART 3: THREE TYPES OF PARAGRAPHS

### 1. Expository Paragraph
**What it is:** Analyzes, explains, evaluates, illustrates, compares, defines, or argues. Answers What? Why? How?

**Used in:** Academic essays, research articles, exam answers, opinion pieces, reports

**Topics that demand expository paragraphs:**
- Happiness, patriotism, discipline, democracy, drug addiction
- "The ballot is stronger than the bullet"
- "Power corrupts"
- "Money is a good servant but a bad master"
- Any topic where you analyze, argue, or explain

**Key features:** Clear beginning and ending; topic sentence is usually explicit; ideas are developed through reasoning, examples, and evidence.

### 2. Descriptive Paragraph
**What it is:** Conveys the writer's sensory perception — what is seen, heard, felt, tasted, smelled, imagined. Does NOT analyze or argue.

**Two Types:**
- **Subjective Description**: Writer's personal, emotional perception of reality. Two writers will describe the same thing differently.
- **Objective Description**: Factual representation of reality as it is. Scientific reports use this.

**Used in:** Stories, novels, autobiographies, scientific observation reports, personal essays

**Topics that demand descriptive paragraphs:**
- A rainy day, traffic jam, a moonlit night, a village fair

**Two types of Descriptive paragraph (from the book):**

| Type | Definition | Result |
|------|-----------|--------|
| **Subjective description** | Writer describes reality *as they perceive it* — coloured by feeling, mood, personality | Two writers on the same subject will write differently |
| **Objective description** | Writer describes reality *as it is* — factual, neutral, without personal colouring | Two writers on the same subject will write similarly |

> **Distinguish at planning stage:** Ask — *Am I describing my experience of this thing (subjective) or the thing itself as it is (objective)?*
> - Traffic jam as *you experienced it this morning* → subjective
> - Traffic jam as *a social phenomenon* → objective (→ becomes expository)

**Key rule:** Descriptive paragraphs convey **sensory perception** (sight, sound, smell, taste, feel). They do NOT analyze, argue, or evaluate — that is expository territory.
- "Her eyes," "A sunset scene," "The girl I saw"

**Vocabulary for description** — you must know words that describe:
shape, color, sound, smell, taste, movement, texture, size, time, position, emotion, gesture, posture, light, temperature, and human qualities.

**Key rule:** Description does not tell what the writer *knows or believes* — it tells what they *sense or imagine*. The moment analysis enters, it becomes expository.

### 3. Narrative Paragraph
**What it is:** Records events in meaningful time sequence. A series of events, not a series of perceptions.

**The Core Distinction:**
- "A Rainy Day" → Descriptive (you see the whole picture, not sequence)
- "How I Spent a Rainy Day" → Narrative (you follow events in time)

**Used in:** Stories, history, autobiography, biography, reports of events

**Key feature:** Driven by chronological (time) order; events unfold one after another; often supported by description.

**Narrative + Description:** Most powerful narratives combine both — events are narrated (narrative), but vivid details are inserted (description) to make them live.

## PART 4: PARAGRAPH DEVELOPMENT METHODS

After identifying the type and order, develop the paragraph using one or more of these methods:

### Method 1: Definition *(Most widely used for expository paragraphs)*
Use when explaining an abstract concept, term, or idea. Decide the definition strategy *before* writing.

**Two levels of definition:**
- **Nominal (dictionary)** — define the word itself (etymology, synonym, or dictionary meaning)
- **Real** — define the concept or thing the word refers to

Often a paragraph begins with a nominal definition, then builds toward a real definition.
Start with: *"The dictionary definition of X is..."* then qualify or expand it.

**Six types of real definition:**

| Type | What It Does | Example Opening |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Consensual** | How people commonly understand it | "Normally, we mean by X..." |
| **Stipulative** | Special meaning assigned for writer's convenience | "For the purposes of this essay, X will mean..." |
| **Legislative** | What something *ought* to mean — writer's claim | "A true X is one that..." |
| **Negative** | Define by what it is NOT | "X is not merely... it is..." |
| **By characteristics** | List the defining traits | "X is characterised by..." |
| **By comparison/contrast** | Liken to or distinguish from something known | "X resembles Y in that... but differs in..." |

**Classic conceptual structure for a definition paragraph:**
```
1. Consensual definition (how people commonly understand it)
2. BUT — show limitations of that definition (with examples/reality)
3. THEREFORE — legislative definition (what it REALLY means)
```

**Seal with an epigram** — definition paragraphs land hardest when they end with a pithy, memorable sentence:
- *"Your character is what you do in the dark."*
- *"An ideal teacher is one who makes himself unnecessary to his pupils."*

> ⚠️ Rule: If the word is already clear to your reader, skip nominal definition entirely — go straight to the real definition.

---

### Method 2: Enumeration (Listing)
Used to list parallel points supporting the topic sentence.
Use **open enumeration** (first, second, third...) or **closed enumeration** (finally, in all...).

### Method 3: Comparison and Contrast
**Pure Comparison:** Both things share similar features
**Pure Contrast:** The things differ fundamentally
**Two-Phase Contrast:** Present one side, then the other (connected by: *but, yet, however, on the other hand*)
**Chain Contrast:** A > B > C > D (each step compared to the previous)
**Implied Contrast:** The contrast is not stated but is clearly suggested

### Method 4: Cause and Effect
- List all causes and all effects
- Map their relationships (simple or chain)
- Decide: cause → effect order, or effect → cause order?
- Effect → Cause is usually more readable and engaging

### Method 5: Classification
Introduce with:
- "... falls into four categories."
- "... can be divided into..."
- "... may be classified as..."
Then use enumeratives to link categories.

### Method 6: Illustration (Examples)
Use examples to prove or support a generalization.
Introduce with: *for example, for instance, to illustrate, namely, such as, take, say, consider, suppose*

**Positioning "for example":**
- At the start: "For example, when you are depressed, laughter can cheer you up."
- In the middle: "Laughter, for example, can cure depression."
- At the end: "Many diseases can be cured by mood, laughter, for example."
Vary its position — never use it the same way repeatedly.

### Method 7: Description and Narration
- **For description:** Accumulate sensory vocabulary. Build atmosphere and feeling through specific, concrete detail.
- **For narration:** Follow time order strictly. Insert description to make events vivid. Do not confuse time sequence.

## PART 5: PARAGRAPH BUILDING PROCESS

### Step 1 — Identify the Topic Idea
What is this paragraph about? State it in one clause.

### Step 2 — Choose the Topic Sentence Type
Opening (deductive), Closing (inductive), Implied, or Two-Phase?

### Step 3 — Choose the Development Method
Enumeration / Comparison / Cause-Effect / Classification / Illustration / Description-Narration

### Step 4 — Choose the Order
General→Specific / Chronological / Cause→Effect / Effect→Cause / Circular / etc.

### Step 5 — Brainstorm Supporting Points
Apply the I-SEEK model (see Essay Planner): What do I Know? What have I Experienced? What can I Imagine?

### Step 6 — Draft the Paragraph
Write topic sentence → develop body → write clincher.

### Step 7 — Test Against CUCO
- ✅ **C**ompleteness: Is the topic idea fully developed?
- ✅ **U**nity: Does every sentence directly support the topic sentence?
- ✅ **C**oherence: Do ideas flow smoothly?
- ✅ **O**rder: Are ideas in the most logical and effective sequence?

## Rules
- One paragraph = one topic idea. Never allow two distinct ideas in one paragraph.
- Topic sentence completeness determines paragraph completeness — they are locked together.
- The closing sentence of a paragraph should either summarize, clinch, or create a bridge to the next.
- Splitting a paragraph for unity may harm completeness — always check both after splitting.
- For descriptive paragraphs: no analysis. For expository paragraphs: no pure sensory description without analytical purpose.
- Narrative = events in time. Descriptive = perceptions in space/mind. Know the difference.
